History vs. model: Roman civil wars and instability intensified after AD 235. The three routes, earnings, risks, and reporting biases below are fictional model assumptions, not estimated historical data. This is a thought experiment about decisions during regime change.

Historical context: Cambridge Ancient History, “Maximinus to Diocletian and the Crisis”.

What Are We Testing?

Reported evidence
yi,t = xi,t + bi + ηi,t

Real outcome x, reporting bias b, random observation noise η. The decision-maker only receives y.

Risk-adjusted decision
Scorei = Qi − λ si

Estimated return Q minus a penalty for estimated volatility s. λ controls risk aversion.

All strategies use the same underlying risk-sensitive score. They differ in whether and how they explore. Mean–standard deviation is only one risk model; it does not guarantee protection against rare disasters.

Three Decision Strategies

🔒
1 · Risk-aware Exploit

Always use the route with the best estimated risk-adjusted score. No deliberate exploration.

🎲
2 · ε-Greedy Trips

Usually exploit, but sometimes commit to a randomly chosen route and bear its full risk.

🔎
3 · Low-cost Scouting

Usually exploit, but sometimes collect a proxy report on another route for a small cost, without a full trading trip.

Model, regret definition, risk, and limitations

Returns: each trip earns a simulated percentage return with a probability of a severe loss. Return distributions differ by route and, under the crisis condition, change halfway through. Wealth compounds at each decision.

Information: an imperfect report is y = realized route outcome + route-specific reporting bias + Gaussian reporting noise. The agent does not see the true mean, the risk probabilities, or the realized full-route outcome separately; only the report. A scouting decision receives a similarly imperfect proxy report but incurs only a fixed 0.6% wealth cost instead of making a full trading trip.

Beliefs: the agent updates each route's reported mean and a volatility estimate using recent-data weighting α = 0.18. The volatility estimate subtracts the assumed known reporting noise variance; estimates remain unreliable with few observations or large bias. When the confirmation filter is on, negative reports about Route A receive only 25% of the usual learning weight.

Risk: risk-sensitive exploitation chooses the highest estimated mean minus λ times estimated standard deviation. This is a simplified mean–variance penalty, not a constraint on maximum losses, a CVaR objective, or a guarantee of survival.

Regret: at decision t, let Ui,t = E[Xi,t] − λ SD(Xi,t) for each route i, using true simulator distributions. A scouting action has utility −0.6. The instantaneous benchmark is max(UA,t, UB,t, UC,t, −0.6). Cumulative regret is the sum of benchmark utility minus chosen-action utility. The agent cannot calculate this quantity; the simulator uses hidden parameters for evaluation. Regret is in percentage-point utility units, not realized wealth.

Safety: the drawdown frequency counts the fraction of simulated histories that ever fell 30% below their previous wealth peak. The 10th-percentile terminal wealth measures a lower-tail outcome. These are descriptive simulation metrics, not independent safety guarantees.

Experimental design: every strategy receives two initial full trips on each route (same information). For each Monte Carlo run, all strategies face the same pre-generated route outcomes and reporting noise. Strategy-specific exploration choices remain random. Fixed seeds reproduce the demonstration.

Interpretation: “confirmation bias” is represented by a deliberately simplified asymmetric evidence update, not a validated cognitive model. This is a causal simulation of stated assumptions, not evidence that one investment, military, or business strategy always performs best.
