The Cost of Being Certain

When a familiar route becomes dangerous, is it enough to be risk-aware?

Imagine you are a merchant in the Roman Empire. You have two possible trade routes. Road A has served you well for years. Sea Route B is unfamiliar and has a poor reputation. You have 100 gold coins to start.

View model assumptions and bias–variance explanation

Historical setting: a fictional merchant around AD 220–260. Rome experienced a third-century political and military crisis beginning in AD 235; neither these exact trade routes nor their numerical returns are historical data. Routes A/B are pedagogical abstractions.

Fixed setup: 52 trading decisions, starting wealth 100, bankruptcy below 20 coins (trading stops), 500 paired Monte Carlo simulations, risk penalty λ = 0.6. Both agents begin with the same favorable belief about A and pessimistic belief about B. Their estimates update gradually with observed reports.

Peace: A usually gains ~5 coins but can have rare losses. B usually gains ~1.6 coins with some downside. Both have uncertainty. War: after decision 20, A faces frequent severe losses while B becomes more profitable.

Observation variance: trip reports have Gaussian noise with standard deviation 2 coins. Observation bias: in “War + Rumors,” reports from Route A are shifted upward by 7 coins after the crisis, and negative updates concerning the familiar route receive only 20% of their ordinary weight. This is a stylized model of misleading information and confirmation bias.

Scouting: at decisions 1, 5, 9, …, the scouting strategy pays 1.8 coins for an independent, noisy report of Route B’s current expected profit (noise SD 1.2). It also makes one full trading decision that season. Its information source is less biased, but not perfect.

Real outcome vs. information: a realized trade profit changes wealth, while a separate imperfect report updates beliefs. This distinguishes a hidden economic regime from evidence used to forecast future trips. The merchant’s inference model is intentionally simplified; delayed or filtered reports are an abstraction rather than an accurate ancient accounting system.

Regret: the oracle always selects the route with the highest current expected profit. Each actual trip incurs max(μA, μB) − μchosen; scouting adds its direct cost. Once bankrupt, regret also accumulates foregone positive oracle earnings in remaining periods. This is an illustrative opportunity-loss measure, not the same as realized wealth or risk-adjusted utility.

Fair comparison: the same simulated route outcomes and baseline report noise are made available for both strategies in each run; only their choices and additional scouting observations differ. The bias mechanism is a model assumption, not empirical validation of psychological behavior.

Important: parameters were selected to make contrasting mechanisms visible. The simulation does not prove that scouting always wins, or that historical merchants behaved this way.

Historical context: Crisis of the Third Century.
