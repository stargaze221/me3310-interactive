Monty Hall Monte Carlo Explorer

Use repeated simulated games to compare two strategies. Then watch finite-sample win rates converge toward the theoretical probabilities.

The question

You choose one of three doors. One door hides a car; the other two hide goats. Monty knows where the car is and always opens a different door that contains a goat. Should you stay with your original choice or switch to the remaining closed door?

Important assumption: Monty always opens a goat door, never opens your chosen door, and always offers the switch. Under these rules, the theoretical win probabilities are 1/3 for staying and 2/3 for switching.

Why does switching win about two-thirds of the time?

Your first choice is correct only when it initially selects the car, which happens with probability 1/3. If the initial choice is wrong—which happens with probability 2/3—Monty's goat reveal leaves the car behind the only other closed door. Therefore:

Initial choice is correct (1/3) → Stay wins
Initial choice is wrong (2/3) → Switch wins

Monte Carlo does not create this probability law. It gives us repeated evidence that is consistent with it.

Try This

  1. Reset with seed 42 and play 1 detailed game several times. Inspect which strategy wins and why.
  2. Reset again with seed 42. Do the same games reappear?
  3. Run only 10 games. Would you trust the observed win rates as precise estimates?
  4. Run 100, 1,000, then 10,000 games. Watch the cumulative curves approach 1/3 and 2/3.
  5. Change the seed and repeat 10,000 games. The exact sequence changes, but do the engineering conclusions change?
Takeaway: Monte Carlo is useful when one realization can be misleading. Repeated simulated evidence reveals the long-run behavior of a strategy, while a fixed seed makes the computational experiment reproducible.
